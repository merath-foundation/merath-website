010.10 Overview

10 is the craft arm of merath's press, and a sustained research project on the material reality of print and paper in the regions the press operates within. The project works toward a press that is materially anchored in its context — that holds a referenced understanding of the present print infrastructure in Tripoli and the corridor, that carries the long history of paper and ink in the region as a continuous field, that supports and extends the existing craft lineages of conservation and binding alongside a sustained engagement with scientific paper conservation as archival practice, that develops a hybrid printing practice across the technologies and materials it operates directly, and that runs its own working capacity at the rahba for the publications, posters, and prints it produces.

The work organises around three sustained directions that cross and intersect throughout: the history and present infrastructure of print and paper in the trans-Saharan and trans-Mediterranean corridor; the craft research on paper, ink, binding, and type as subjects of study in their own right; and the operational practice of the press's working courtyard, where the publications come into material form alongside the research they extend.

The rahba (رحبة) — the open courtyard at the heart of traditional Maghrebi and Saharan architecture — names the working space and the working principle of the project. The rahba is where craft is practised in the open, where knowledge is transferred through apprenticeship and exchange, where the labour of making sits in sociality. The project takes the rahba as both architectural reference and methodological commitment.

The project is operationally related to 09 Publishing Field / ميدان النشر — which holds the research, editorial decisions, and design-as-argument for the press's publications. 09 decides what a publication is and what it should do; 10 makes it physical and studies the conditions of its making. The two projects together carry the press's working position on what publishing is and what it can do.

010.40 The three research threads

The history and present infrastructure of print and paper in the corridor — the project studies how print and paper have circulated through the trans-Saharan and trans-Mediterranean corridor across centuries, and the print infrastructure that currently holds in Tripoli and the wider corridor. The historical and the present feed each other: every working printer in Tripoli today carries some inheritance from earlier infrastructures, and every paper in the city has come through trade routes that have operated for centuries.

The craft research on paper, ink, binding, and type as subjects of study in their own right. Paper as material and as historical object across the corridor's history, gathered into the rahba's paper library as a working resource. Ink as ecology, both in its botanical/mineral/animal sources gathered into the natural pigment library, and in the print-colour culture of the modern period. Binding as inherited craft and conservation as both traditional and scientific practice — the Maghrebi tradition, the Quranic codex sewing practices, the restoration networks in Tripoli the press apprentices into, the press's own working sewing team and its developing range, the scientific paper conservation work that feeds artistic and archival registers, the relationship between paper, signatures, and sewing technique. Type as historical and contemporary practice — the Maghrebi script lineages, type design for bilingual setting.

The operational practice of the working courtyard. The rahba in Tripoli is the working site where the publications, posters, and prints come into physical form and where the craft research becomes operational — the production runs, the test prints, the apprenticeship and workshop pedagogy, the documentation of craft processes, the conservation of the press's own publications and partner archive material, and the material accession system that tracks every batch of paper, ink, binding stock, and type set against the publications they enter.

010.50 The Tripoli print-shop database

The Tripoli print-shop database is the project's developing referenced map of the present infrastructure of commercial and artisanal printing in the city. The work is surveying every operational printer in Tripoli — locating, visiting, documenting. Each shop enters the database with its location and provenance, its machine capacities, its paper holdings and stock conditions, its finishing capabilities, its lineage, its working conditions. The database functions as research output (a referenced map of the city's print infrastructure) and as working resource (the press's operational knowledge of where to route what production).

The historical layer holds in parallel. The Italian-era presses that printed colonial material and post-independence first runs. The state-press apparatus that operated from independence through the Gaddafist period, with its specific machines, paper supplies, and editorial gatekeeping. The Gaddafist-era restrictions and the ways small operations worked around them. The post-2011 fragmentation and the new shops that have emerged. Every operational shop carries some inheritance from these earlier phases, and the database documents that inheritance alongside the contemporary working condition. A working print specimen library is accumulating — examples of historical Tripoli and Libyan printing collected from older shops, family collections, and archives — that documents the lineages the contemporary infrastructure has inherited.

The Tripoli paper database holds inside the broader survey. The work is documenting what paper types currently sit in Tripoli's stationery stores and import channels, what the import routes are, what stocks of older papers sit in the back rooms of long-standing shops and warehouses. The paper database connects to the press's accession system and feeds both the history-of-paper research and the paper library the rahba is assembling.

010.60 The history of paper and the economies of the…

The history-of-paper research has opened as a sustained study of paper as a continuous material-historical object that has moved through the trans-Saharan and trans-Mediterranean corridor for over a thousand years.

The project takes up the existing scholarship on the fatwa and the economies of the paper trade as one of its anchoring threads. The fatwa material — the religious and legal frameworks through which paper was acquired, valued, certified for use in scholarly and Quranic copying, and circulated across the corridor — sits as a research field the project draws on and extends. The merchant and waqf economies that financed the trade are studied alongside the fatwa material as continuous infrastructure: the religious-economic relation to paper carried the trade as much as the physical caravans did.

The project has begun to assemble the early arrival of paper in the Islamic world — Samarkand in the eighth century, then Baghdad, Damascus, Fustat, and onward through the Maghreb. The Fes paper-mill tradition that operated from the eleventh and twelfth centuries, with its connection to the Andalusian paper centres at Xàtiva and Valencia. The Mediterranean paper trade that moved Maghrebi and Andalusian paper across the corridor before the Italian paper transition of the thirteenth century, when Fabriano and other Italian mills came to anchor the Mediterranean supply.

The research traces the trans-Saharan paper routes that carried paper south from the Maghreb to the Sahel — the routes through Ghadames, Ouargla, and Sijilmasa to Timbuktu and beyond, where the great Saharan manuscript culture depended on paper that had travelled across the desert. Ghadames sits at the centre of the project as a node in the trans-Saharan paper trade and as a continuing site of manuscript and bookbinding craft. The active relation with 02 Saharan Currency — holds here: the merchant houses, the waqf endowments, and the credit-and-surety arrangements that organised the paper trade are the same infrastructures 02 studies on the currency side, and the projects feed each other's research.

The research extends into the Ottoman paper economy and its transitions, the colonial paper economy of the Italian period, the post-independence and Gaddafist state-press paper supplies, and the current import routes — each studied for what it carried, who controlled it, what crafts depended on it.

010.70 Paper conservation

The conservation work holds in active relation with the restoration networks in Tripoli — including the women in the city who carry the Quranic codex restoration craft for Qurans and masahif. The press's apprenticeship into these networks is structured by the practitioners' own terms. The aim of the relation is twofold: to support the existing lineage as it continues its conservation work, and to extend the techniques the lineage carries into sewing applications for merath's non-religious publications, so that the craft moves into broader contemporary use alongside its continuing Quranic conservation.

Alongside the working relation with the restoration networks, the project carries a sustained engagement with scientific paper conservation as a research and practice field. The scientific conservation work — material analysis (paper fibre composition, sizing, ink chemistry), environmental protocols (temperature, humidity, light), treatment protocols (deacidification, washing, lining, mending), digitisation standards, and storage protocols — feeds the press's practice in two registers. As artistic practice, the scientific knowledge of paper's material behaviour feeds the press's design and production decisions: which paper to choose for which publication, how the ink will sit on the paper over decades, how the binding will hold the paper as it ages, how each publication will sit as an archival object. As serious archival concern, the scientific conservation knowledge serves the preservation of historical material, of politically sensitive material that needs to endure beyond the present, of family collections including the Ghadames libraries, and of the press's own publications as material objects with their own future life. The scientific conservation work sits alongside the traditional craft conservation as complementary practice — the two registers together holding the work the project carries forward.

The press's own working team of sewers has developed through this apprenticeship and through the press's accumulating publication work. The team operates at the rahba and in working relation with the broader restoration networks in the city. Its capacities span: traditional Maghrebi sewing techniques across single- and multi-signature codices; the Quranic codex sewing protocols the conservation lineages carry; the Alpha Stitch developed for the Shafawī series; and the developing sewing forms that emerge as each series surfaces its specific binding needs. The team carries the binding production for the press's own publications and contributes to the conservation work the press shares with partner libraries and the restoration networks. The team's range continues to extend with each volume.

The Quranic codex sewing practices carry the operational knowledge for stabilising, restoring, and continuing the use of older paper works. The sewing protocols — the gathering structure, the thread choice, the stitching pattern, the spine treatment — have been preserved through generations of practitioners who learned the work as apprentices to those who learned it the same way. Each sewing pattern carries an argument about how the codex is meant to be used, how it is meant to endure, how it is meant to be opened and read. The Maghrebi codex tradition holds its own distinctive structure, and the project is studying its variants across the corridor — the Tripolitanian, the Saharan, the Fes lineages — as continuous craft material.

The relationship between paper type, number of signatures, and sewing technique sits at the centre of the binding research. Different paper weights and grains call for different gathering structures; different signature counts call for different stitch patterns; different sewing techniques produce different opening behaviours and different durability profiles. The research traces these relationships through the historical material and develops them into the press's own binding methodology.

The Alpha Stitch is the press's developed sewing form for the Shafawī / Feminist Oral History series. Bound as a two-signature codex, the Alpha Stitch produces the opening angle, weight, and material register the series calls for, and ties the binding directly to the editorial reading protocol. The technique extends as one of the press's working sewing forms, with each subsequent volume contributing to its development.

The conservation work bridges into the wider merath research. The active relation with 02 Saharan Currency — holds at the level of the merchant and waqf infrastructures the paper-trade research and the conservation lineages both touch. The active relation with 12 Musical Manuscripts — holds at the level of the conservation networks themselves; 12's research on music manuscripts in private collections draws on the same restoration networks and the same Quranic-codex craft, with the bridge operational as well as methodological, and the scientific conservation work directly serving 12's archival needs.

010.80 The ecology of ink and dyes

The ecology-of-ink-and-dyes research has opened as a sustained ecological study. Ink and dye are continuous phenomena that hold ecology, geography, economy, and craft in single objects.

The botanical sources sit at one centre. Pomegranate rind, walnut shell and husk, gall nuts, henna, indigo, madder, kermes, saffron, turmeric, sumac — each carries its own ecology, its own geography of cultivation, its own trade route through the corridor. The mineral sources sit at another — lamp-black and soot, earth pigments (ochre, sienna, umber), lapis lazuli from Afghan supply, azurite. The animal sources extend the work — cochineal moving across the Mediterranean from later Spanish trade, sepia from cuttlefish. The research traces each colourant through its plant or mineral or animal source, through the trade route that carried it, through the merchant economy that organised it, and through the craft formulation that turned it into ink.

The natural pigment library is the working extension of the research. The press is assembling a collection of pigments at the rahba — sources gathered from the corridor's botanical, mineral, and animal supply, held for printing and for the colour-research the ecology project carries. Each pigment in the library is entered with its source, its provenance (region of origin, supplier or harvest condition, season), and its working properties (lightfastness, hue range, binder compatibility, age behaviour). The library sits as both research collection and working print resource: pigments that can be formulated into inks for specific publications, posters, and prints, with the formulation work itself feeding back into the research record. The connection to broader regional plant and material knowledge — textile dyeing, medicine, cuisine — holds in the library as continuous field.

The print-colour culture of the regional twentieth century opens as a parallel research thread. The palettes that state-press, oppositional, small-press, and commercial production carried across the colonial, post-independence, Gaddafist, and post-2011 periods. The specific blues and reds and ochres that became politically and culturally legible — the colours of state propaganda, the colours of oppositional samizdat-style print, the colours of the commercial paper-and-ink market, the colours of the children's-book and educational sectors that carried their own palettes. The project is assembling the colour catalogue of the modern Libyan and regional print culture, and the catalogue feeds back into the press's own colour decisions and into the natural pigment library's working formulations.

The connection to the classical Arabic al-midād tradition is sustained. The calligraphic schools that codified ink-making as a craft preserved knowledge the contemporary research recovers and adapts. The lineages of Quranic conservation that the restoration networks in Tripoli carry hold the operational knowledge of which inks endure, which fade, which carry archival weight, and the work apprentices into that knowledge.

010.90 The rahba's print capacity

The rahba's print capacity has developed deliberately as a hybrid printing practice. The press's publications, posters, and prints combine technologies and materials across single objects rather than committing to single processes: riso layered with CMYK, hand printing over digital, receipt-printer strips bound into codex publications, over- and under-printing across multiple machines. The hybrid approach lets the press make the material decision at the level of each layer of each publication, and lets the design argument from 09 land in print at a register that no single technology could carry alone. The hybrid practice continues to develop as the press extends its technologies, materials, and methods.

The risograph carries the press's small-run and chapbook production. Low cost, layered colour, the surface quality particular to the artists' press tradition. The risograph development at the rahba has extended through deepening operation — the colour separations, the registration, the paper choices, the layered effects that the over- and under-printing practice opens. The risograph combines with the other technologies in hybrid work, with riso layers carrying alongside CMYK and hand passes.

The CMYK printer holds two ink streams — dye-based and pigment-based. Dye-based CMYK carries the vibrant colour spectrum the press uses for publications that depend on saturation and visual immediacy; pigment-based CMYK carries the archival weight, the lightfastness, the longer durability. Holding both lets the press make the material decision about each publication: which ink stream serves which design argument, and how the two streams layer in hybrid work.

The receipt printer sits as a working capacity for publications, posters, and prints where the immediate-impermanent register is the design argument. The printer uses heat to develop the image, producing a particular form — the narrow strip, the surface that fades and shifts with time, the relation to the contemporary economy of slips and receipts. The rahba holds varied thermal paper rolls in different stocks, weights, and surface qualities, each stock studied for what it brings. The receipt printer combines into hybrid objects, with receipt-printed strips bound, layered, or affixed into larger printed works.

The hand-printing techniques the press has been building carry the slow embodied production. Manual ink application, hand-stamping, hand-applied colour, the techniques developed alongside the test-print practice and producing one-off and limited-run works where the human hand is part of the editorial argument. The hand work layers into the hybrid practice — hand passes over riso, over CMYK, over receipt, producing the specific registration and material register the press's publications, posters, and prints carry.

Over- and under-printing runs across the technologies. The riso over- and under-prints across its colour separations; the CMYK printer over- and under-prints across its dye and pigment streams; the hand printing over- and under-prints across its manual layers; the technologies over- and under-print into each other. The practice produces layered colour, registration shift, and palimpsest effects the press uses as a research direction.

The paper library is the rahba's collected working resource of papers, assembled across the Tripoli market, the regional supply chains, and the trans-regional network. The library carries paper variety that the limited Libyan paper market does not currently hold — the variety of weights, surfaces, finishes, colours, and provenances the press's design arguments call for. The reflection that anchors the library is honest: Libya's paper supply has been restricted by import dependencies, by the contraction of the post-2011 commercial sector, by the loss of older paper-trade routes. The library is the press's working response to that condition, gathering the variety from across the corridor that Tripoli's market does not currently offer, and holding it at the rahba against the publications it will enter. Every paper in the library is entered into the material accession system with its provenance, its working condition, and the publications it enters.

The natural pigment library extends alongside the paper library as the rahba's collected working resource of colourants — described in the ecology-of-ink-and-dyes section above. The library carries the botanical, mineral, and animal sources gathered for printing and for the colour research, formulated into working inks the press uses across its publications, posters, and prints.

Posters and prints sit alongside the press's series publications as outputs of the rahba's production. The press produces posters for its exhibitions and announcements, document-prints that hold archival material in single-sheet form, limited-edition prints that emerge from the over- and under-printing practice and from the hand work, and the test prints that accumulate as their own object class. The posters and prints carry the hybrid printing practice as fully as the codex publications do — single sheets that combine riso, CMYK, hand, and over/under work in single objects.

Test prints sit at the centre of the working method. Every publication, every paper, every ink combination, every binding decision, every poster and print is tested before the full run, and often after the run as the press examines what worked and what could be extended. The test prints accumulate as a working record of what the press has tried, and feed into the craft documentation protocol. The test-print practice is one of the principal sites where the publication-design argument from 09 meets the material reality of print at 10.

The longer-run production the rahba's direct capacity does not carry — large-edition offset, lithograph runs, specific finishing — happens through the trans-regional print network and the Tripoli commercial printing infrastructure the print-shop database maps.

010.100 The trans-regional print network

The trans-regional print network across the corridor is the operational extension of 09's Trans-Saharan-Mediterranean Book Trade research and the present-day subject of its own sustained study. Where 09 studies the historical condition of cross-regional print, 10 studies the present-day infrastructure that the corridor's history made possible and continues to shape, and operates within that infrastructure for the press's own production.

The network is anchored on working relations across the corridor — in Tunis, Algiers, Cairo, and the further nodes the network extends into (Khartoum, Casablanca, Damascus, Beirut). Each node carries its own infrastructure: the Tunisian small-press culture with its strong offset capacity and active art-book scene; the Algerian lineages of independent print that carry methodological knowledge from the liberation-era infrastructure; the Cairene regional printing hub with its scale and breadth of materials sourcing; the further nodes that open as the network thickens. The network also feeds the rahba's paper and pigment libraries — papers and pigments gathered from across the corridor enter the libraries through the network's working relations.

The research studies the network itself as continuing infrastructure. The trust economies that hold the network together — reputational vetting, ritualised handover, the friendships that anchor working relations — are studied as the present-day instances of the social technologies that 09's research identifies in historical material. The cellular structure that the network operates with, the redundancy through distributed production, the route-based rather than hub-based logic of cross-regional craft — these are continuing features of how print works across the corridor, and the work treats them as research material as well as operational practice.

The historical lessons that 09 surfaces become operational in the network: the cellular structure, the trusted couriers, the ritualised handover points, the redundancy through distributed production. The two projects together carry the corridor's continuous print history as both research subject and continuing practice.

010.110 Craft methodology

The craft work that runs across every production and publication, held distinct from the editorial methodology at 09 and from the research methodology at 04. Four practices articulate the methodology.

The material accession system — every batch of paper, every batch of ink and pigment, every binding stock, every type set entered into a tracked register with origin, supplier, date of acquisition, working condition, and the publications it enters. The system holds the press's material history as continuous record, and feeds the paper library and the natural pigment library as their working catalogues.

The craft documentation protocol — every craft process documented in working form: paper-making batches with their material composition, ink formulations with their botanical and mineral sources, binding procedures with their structural notes, printing runs with their machine settings and paper choices, hybrid work with its layering and registration records, conservation interventions with their before-and-after records (across both traditional and scientific registers), and the test prints that anchor each production. The documentation holds the craft as transmissible knowledge.

The apprenticeship and workshop pedagogy — the rahba as a teaching space. Workshops in paper-making, ink-making, binding, risograph operation, and conservation, opened to practitioners and to wider publics. Apprenticeship relations with the restoration networks in Tripoli, with traditional binders and calligraphers, and with conservation practitioners across the corridor structure the press's own learning and the workshops it offers. The sewing team's developing range is itself part of the pedagogy, with the team's work entering the workshops as teaching material.

The conservation practice — the conservation of the press's own publications, the conservation work the press contributes to partner libraries, archives, and the workshops of the restoration networks in Tripoli, the scientific conservation work carried as archival practice for historical material, and the conservation work that bridges into 12 Musical Manuscripts — through the shared lineages and the scientific archival protocols both projects need.

010.120 A reflection

The press operates from a particular position on craft. Material craft in the regions merath works — Libya in particular, the Sahara and the Mediterranean more broadly — has been carried by lineages of practitioners working within specific historical conditions: colonial print infrastructure, post-independence state apparatus, Gaddafist-era restrictions, post-2011 fragmentation, and the continuing infrastructural conditions that shape what can currently be made. The press takes the craft as subject of research and as working environment of practice. The two are continuous.

The rahba names the architectural and methodological commitment. The open courtyard of craft, where labour sits in sociality, where knowledge is transferred through apprenticeship and exchange, where the publications come into material form alongside the practitioners who make them. The rahba holds the position that material craft is a living practice continuously held in transmission, and that the press takes its place inside that transmission and works to extend it.

The project's working aims are concrete: a referenced map of the print infrastructure that holds Tripoli and the corridor; a continuous history-of-paper research field that anchors the press's understanding of its material context; the active support of the conservation craft lineages and the extension of their techniques into the press's own publications, alongside the scientific paper conservation work that feeds the press's artistic practice and its archival concerns; a working sewing team at the rahba whose range continues to extend with each series; the paper library and the natural pigment library as the rahba's gathered material resources held against the limits of the Libyan paper market and against the working colour needs of the press; the hybrid printing practice across the technologies the press operates directly, for the publications, posters, and prints the rahba produces; the trans-regional print network as live operational and research infrastructure; and a developed workshop pedagogy that returns to the regional craft transmission what the press has been able to learn.

The relation to 09 holds the press whole. 09 studies and decides; 10 makes and studies the conditions of making. The publications move between them as they pass through the design and the material phases of their coming into being. The two projects together carry the press's working position on what publishing is and what it can do.
